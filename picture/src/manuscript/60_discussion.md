## 5. Discussion: what should a policy predict?

World action models (WAMs) generate future video together with actions and are argued to generalise better than VLAs because prediction makes them learn dynamics [@dreamzero; @wamstudy]. We do not see prediction as the dividing line between the two families. The open questions are what to predict and when, and the WAM literature states them itself. Fast-WAM keeps video co-training but skips future prediction at test time and remains competitive, whereas removing video co-training costs much more; the value of video prediction “may lie in improving world representations during training” [@fastwam]. The GR00T 2 whitepaper observes that most predicted tokens encode future video, “much of which does not bear on the next action”, calls it unclear “whether prediction helps mainly as a training signal or also as imagination at inference time”, and lists as open “how to add force and touch as inputs without eroding visual priors, and at which control rate” [@gr00t2].

muscleVLTA gives one answer to each question for the regime of contact. *What to predict:* the next arm force and fingertip readings, a few dozen numbers, in the modality that carries the outcome of an occluded insertion; the camera views of that phase show the gripper and the plug, not the socket. *When:* in training only. The forward model is discarded at deployment, a reactive update takes about 60 ms, and the attribution of Section 4.3 suggests that the benefit of touch reaches the policy through this training signal rather than through its test-time input. *How to add force and touch:* through a reactive expert whose cross-attention to the sensor memory starts at zero, so that training starts from the pre-trained policy, at 10 Hz and under an explicit latency of 0.3 s. Our attribution adds a caution that holds for either family: an added input can be exploited as a shortcut [@dehaan; @geirhos], here the force level as a posture cue, and a success rate does not reveal it.

Force prediction and video prediction are therefore complementary. A video model learns how scenes evolve; a force–touch forward model learns how contact responds to commands. The same objective could supervise a WAM, where touch has begun to enter [@tactilewam], and a VLA trained with it obtains predictive supervision without a video backbone.
